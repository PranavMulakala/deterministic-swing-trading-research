The big picture

An option is a contract about 100 shares of a stock. It has a price (the strike) and a deadline (the expiration).

  • A call is the right to buy 100 shares at the strike.
  • A put is the right to sell 100 shares at the strike.
  • The buyer pays the seller cash upfront, called the premium.

There are two sides to every option:

1. Covered call: your AMZN trade

In one sentence: you own 100 shares and sell someone the right to buy them at a higher price. They pay you cash now, and you keep it no matter what.

  1. Buy the shares. 200 AMZN at $259.69 = $51,938.
  2. Sell the calls. 2 × Nov 13 $275 call at $6.85 = $1,370 in your pocket today.
  3. Wait for Nov 13. If AMZN is below $275, the calls expire worthless: you keep the shares and the $1,370, then sell new calls. If AMZN is above $275, your shares sell at $275 automatically ("called away").

2. Rolling: Joey's main skill

Rolling means buying back the call you sold and selling a new one in the same order, usually further out in time. You do it to change the deal without closing the whole trade.

What Joey does: he says "the beauty is I don't close it for a loss. I just roll my call out or I buy a put for protection." 3. Collar: covered call + insurance

In one sentence: a covered call where you also buy a put below the price, so a crash can't hurt you much.

  • The call's premium pays for some or all of the put.
  • Joey did this on MU before earnings ("I just had a PUT on for earnings so if it dropped I capped losses").
CollarCovered call alone

An example on your AMZN trade, with made-up put prices: also buy 2 Nov 13 $245 puts at about $5. Below $245 the loss stops at about −$3,940, instead of getting worse and worse. The cost: your best case drops from +$4,432 to about +$3,432.

When Joey uses it: only when he truly thinks the stock is heading down, or before a risky event like earnings. He uses an in-the-money put about 30 days out, and he sells it back if the worry passes.

4. Diagonal: Joey's MU trade today

In one sentence: a covered call where, instead of 100 shares, you own a long-dated call, and you sell short-dated calls against it.

  • It needs far less money than buying shares.
  • It's also called a "poor man's covered call".

Joey's version today: he sold the MU $1,120 call expiring Fri Oct 9 for $4.95, and owns the MU $1,520 call expiring Jan 15, 2027 (bought at $19.57). He says the best case is about +$970 if MU closes right at $1,120 on Friday, with a 57% chance of the trade making money.

Traced from Joey's thinkorswim chart (approximate). The profit zone on Friday is about $1,051 to $1,132. Below that, he loses about what he paid. The danger is a fast rally: his long call is way up at $1,520, so above about $1,132 the sold $1,120 call loses money quickly.

5. Selling puts (the wheel)

In one sentence: you get paid to promise to buy a stock at a lower price.

  • If the stock stays above the strike, you keep the cash.
  • If it drops below, you buy 100 shares at the strike, and then you can start covered calls on them. That loop is called the wheel.

An illustration: sell one $100 put for $4 on a stock at $108. You keep $400 if it stays above $100. If it falls to $70, you lose $2,600. That's the same risk as owning the shares from $96.

Joey's two conditions: he would "never recommend" selling puts except on stocks under $50 (so even a total collapse costs at most $5,000), or on S&P 500 stocks with a 90%+ chance of the put expiring worthless. Our test #10 sold SPY puts: wins in 76% of weeks, but only about $104 a week on $100k.
